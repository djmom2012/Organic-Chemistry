Catalytic Hydrogenation

How H₂ adds to unsaturated molecules, why benzene resists hydrogenation, and how catalyst choice changes what reacts.

1. The core idea

Catalytic hydrogenation reduces a multiple bond by adding hydrogen from H₂ to a substrate, typically using a metal surface. It is commonly used to convert alkenes to alkanes and, under suitable conditions, aromatic rings to saturated rings.

WHAT CHANGES?

Bond order decreases

An alkene C=C becomes C–C after one equivalent of H₂ is added. Each alkene carbon gains one H.

WHAT THE CATALYST DOES

Lowers the kinetic barrier

The metal surface adsorbs H₂ and the substrate, helps activate H–H, and enables hydrogen transfer. The catalyst is regenerated overall.

Catalysts change the reaction pathway and rate; they do not change the reaction’s equilibrium position.

GENERAL PATTERN

Addition across π bonds

For a simple alkene, one mole of H₂ is consumed per C=C reduced.

Important: Hydrogenation is a reduction: the carbon atoms generally gain C–H bonds and lose π-bond character.

2. Why benzene is unusually stable

Benzene is not best understood as an ordinary cyclohexatriene with three isolated double bonds. Its six π electrons are delocalized around a planar, cyclic conjugated ring.

Benzene: delocalized π system

  • Planar and cyclic: p orbitals can overlap continuously around the ring.
  • Conjugated: every ring carbon contributes a p orbital.
  • Hückel rule: aromatic systems have 4n + 2 π electrons; benzene has 6 (n = 1).

Cyclohexadiene: not aromatic

In cyclohexa-1,3-diene, only four ring carbons participate in the conjugated diene π system; the remaining sp³ carbon interrupts cyclic overlap. It is therefore not aromatic.

Takeaway: Benzene’s extra stability comes from aromatic delocalization. That stability makes addition reactions that destroy aromaticity less favorable than ordinary alkene hydrogenation.

Aromaticity checklist

1
Cyclic

The π system forms a ring.

2
Planar and fully conjugated

A p orbital is available at every atom in the ring.

3
4n + 2 π electrons

The count must fit n = 0, 1, 2… for a conventional aromatic system.

Aromaticity is a combined structural and electronic criterion—not just “having alternating double bonds.”

3. Key hydrogenation examples

The same reagent, H₂, can give different practical outcomes depending on the substrate and conditions. The diagrams below are reaction summaries, not experimental procedures.

Example A — benzene → cyclohexane

3 H₂
⟶metal catalyst; often elevated T/P

Net equation: C₆H₆ + 3 H₂ → C₆H₁₂

Benzene requires three equivalents of H₂ to saturate all three π-bond equivalents. This transformation sacrifices aromaticity, so it commonly requires more forcing conditions than alkene hydrogenation. Industrially and in laboratory contexts, conditions depend strongly on catalyst, pressure, temperature, and substrate.

Example B — cyclohexa-1,3-diene → cyclohexane

2 H₂
⟶suitable metal catalyst

Net equation: C₆H₈ + 2 H₂ → C₆H₁₂

A cyclohexadiene has two C=C bonds, so full saturation requires two equivalents of H₂. The diene is nonaromatic; its π bonds are generally easier to hydrogenate than benzene’s aromatic system.

Example C — cyclohexene → cyclohexane

1 H₂
⟶commonly Pd, Pt, or Ni

Net equation: C₆H₁₀ + H₂ → C₆H₁₂

One isolated alkene C=C usually consumes one equivalent of H₂. This is the standard baseline for comparing more complex unsaturated substrates.

Substrate selectivity matters. A molecule containing both an aromatic ring and another alkene may hydrogenate at the alkene first under many conditions, while the aromatic ring remains intact. This is a useful tendency, not a universal guarantee.

4. Catalyst selection: a practical conceptual guide

Choose a catalyst based on the bond you want to reduce, functional-group compatibility, selectivity, and the conditions a substrate can tolerate. No catalyst is universally “best.”

Decision sequence

  1. Identify the target bond: alkene, alkyne, aromatic ring, or another reducible group.
  2. List other groups that must remain unchanged (carbonyls, halides, nitro groups, protecting groups, sulfur-containing groups, etc.).
  3. Decide whether complete or partial reduction is desired.
  4. Use literature precedent for the closest substrate, then evaluate catalyst, solvent, temperature, and H₂ pressure together.
  5. Confirm product identity and selectivity analytically.

Safety and handling

Hydrogen is flammable; hydrogenation can be exothermic; and supported metal catalysts may be hazardous, especially if allowed to dry after use. Pyrophoricity and handling risks depend on catalyst formulation and state.

This guide is conceptual, not an operating procedure. Follow approved lab procedures, risk assessments, equipment limits, and the specific catalyst supplier’s safety data. Never improvise pressurized hydrogenation equipment.

5. Rules of thumb & key takeaways

1 : 1

Alkene rule

One C=C bond typically consumes one mole of H₂ when fully reduced to C–C.

3 H₂

Benzene rule

Complete benzene-to-cyclohexane reduction consumes three moles of H₂ per mole of benzene.

4n + 2

Aromaticity rule

A planar, cyclic, fully conjugated π system with 4n + 2 π electrons is aromatic under Hückel’s rule.

Remember

  • Aromatic stabilization makes benzene less reactive toward addition than an ordinary alkene.
  • Hydrogenation of benzene destroys aromaticity; stronger conditions are often needed.
  • Cyclohexadiene is nonaromatic because conjugation does not extend around the whole ring.
  • Catalyst identity alone does not determine the product: conditions and substrate structure matter.

Common misconceptions

  • “Benzene has three ordinary alkenes.” Its π electrons are delocalized, and all six C–C bonds are equivalent in the idealized structure.
  • “A catalyst changes equilibrium.” It changes the rate/pathway, not the equilibrium constant.
  • “More active is always better.” Higher activity may reduce selectivity or affect sensitive functional groups.
  • “A ring with double bonds is aromatic.” Cyclic conjugation, geometry, and π-electron count all matter.

6. Quick knowledge check

Open each card to test whether the key ideas have stuck.

How many equivalents of H₂ are needed to convert benzene to cyclohexane?

Three equivalents. Benzene’s formula changes from C₆H₆ to C₆H₁₂, adding six H atoms total.

Why is cyclohexa-1,3-diene not aromatic?

It does not have a continuous cyclic p-orbital system: an sp³ carbon interrupts conjugation around the ring.

Does Pd/C always leave an aromatic ring untouched?

No. Many conditions reduce ordinary alkenes preferentially, but outcomes depend on the substrate, catalyst, pressure, temperature, solvent, and reaction time.

What is the most important first question when selecting a hydrogenation catalyst?

Which bond must be reduced—and which other functional groups must remain unchanged?
